# Worked example 4 — a redacted voice profile

**SYNTHETIC EXAMPLE. Invented owner, invented sample, invented patterns.** This
is what `voice/external.md` looks like for one made-up business owner after she
read it and made her edits.

The most useful thing about this file is **what is not in it**. Read the
"what this file deliberately does not contain" section at the bottom.

---

## How it got built

**Selection interview.** Priya was asked which sent mail should feed her
outside-the-company voice. She said her Sent folder, and the kit put a date on
it rather than reading everything she had ever written:

> "Let's put a date on it — the last six months, say. Writing drifts, and old
> mail describes somebody you used to be. It also means I'm reading a lot less
> of your mail than I would otherwise."

**Exclusions.** She excluded anything to her accountant and anything about staff.
Recorded permanently; they hold on every future run.

**Size, said back before anything was read:**

> "That's about six months of your Sent folder — probably sixty messages, and
> I'll only use the ones you actually wrote yourself. Sound right, or do you
> want to narrow it?"

**What was actually read:** 58 messages, 31 usable. 27 discarded — 14 forwards
with nothing added, 9 one-word replies, 4 calendar accepts. Above the floor of
15, so it built.

## The file, as it stands after her edits

```
# Voice profile — external

reviewed by owner:   yes
reviewed on:         2026-08-17
built on:            2026-08-17
built from:          Sent folder, 2026-02-17 to 2026-08-17
                     (excluded: accountant correspondence, anything about staff)
messages sampled:    58 read, 31 usable
                     (27 discarded: 14 forwards, 9 one-word replies, 4 calendar accepts)
sign-off form:       "Thanks," on its own line, then "Priya" on the next

## Greeting
Uses the person's first name and a comma. No greeting word — no "Hi",
no "Hello", no "Good morning". Same form whether it is a first
contact or the twentieth message in a thread.

## Opening move
Straight to the subject in the first sentence. Does not acknowledge
the previous message before answering it, and does not open with a
pleasantry. Where the answer is bad news or a delay, the delay is the
first sentence rather than the third.

Typical opening shape (invented example, not from your mail):
"Sam, the part is three weeks out."

## Sentence length and rhythm
Short. Most sentences run under fifteen words and almost none run
past twenty-five. Frequent one-sentence paragraphs. No semicolons
anywhere in the sample. Uses dashes to attach a consequence to a
statement rather than starting a new sentence for it.

## Formality
Contractions throughout. First names for everyone including people
addressed formally in their own signature. Direct rather than hedged:
states what will happen rather than what could be arranged. Does not
use "please" as a softener at the start of a request, but does use it
at the end of one.

## Structure
Two to four short paragraphs. No bullets and no numbered lists in the
sample at all, including in messages that listed three or four items —
those were written as sentences. Nothing is bolded. Frequently restates
the ask in the last line before the sign-off.

## How they say no, and how they push
Says no in the first sentence and gives one reason, without softening
it first. Does not apologize for the refusal and does not offer an
alternative unless there genuinely is one.

When chasing something, does not apologize for chasing. States what
is outstanding, states the consequence of it staying outstanding, and
names a date. Does not use "just following up" or "just checking in".

## Commitments and dates
Gives specific days rather than windows — a named weekday, sometimes
with a time. Where the date is not known yet, commits to a date for
knowing it rather than to the thing itself.

## Owner's own corrections
source: stated by owner, 2026-08-17
- Never open with a pleasantry. She was firm about this.
- If it is bad news, it goes first.

## Things they never do
- No exclamation marks anywhere in 31 messages.
- No "I hope this email finds you well".
- No "circling back", "touching base", "reaching out", or
  "just following up".
- No emoji.
- No "Best regards" or "Kind regards" — the sign-off is one word.
- Does not sign with a title or the company name; first name only.

## What I could not tell
Nothing in this sample was a complaint or a dispute — the closest
was a delayed part, which she treated as logistics. I have no read
on how she writes when somebody is angry with her. Drafts in that
situation will play it straight and plain, and she should expect to
rewrite them until she points me at a few.

There was also no first-contact mail to a stranger in the sample;
everything was to somebody she already knew. A cold introduction may
come out warmer than she would write it.
```

## What she changed during her review

The kit's first draft had a line reading *"Occasionally opens with a short
pleasantry before the subject."* She struck it:

> "That's not me, that's me being polite to two people. Cut it."

It was cut, and recorded under `## Owner's own corrections` instead. **The
owner's description of their own voice outranks the sample every time** — she
has written every email she ever sent; the kit read 31.

Until she said the file was right, `reviewed by owner:` stayed `no`, and the
drafting skill **refused to write anything with it**. That refusal is in
`skills/draft-replies/SKILL.md` and it is not adjustable.

## What this file deliberately does not contain

This is the part to check against any other vendor's "voice learning."

- **Not one sentence copied from her mail.** Zero verbatim fragments, including
  in the parts that describe her openings. The one example shown is labeled
  invented and is written on made-up content.
- **No client, customer, or company names.** The sample was 31 real messages to
  real correspondents; none of them appear.
- **No subject lines**, which are usually the job or the matter.
- **No dollar figures, dates, addresses, phone numbers, or invoice numbers**
  from any message.
- **No recipient list**, and nothing indicating who she writes to or how often.
- **Her own first name appears once**, in the sign-off form, because a draft has
  to end somehow.

The test applied to every line before it was written: *if this file were emailed
to a stranger, would it disclose anything about her clients or her business
beyond how she writes?* If yes, the line describes the pattern instead of
showing it.

**Where a pattern genuinely could not be described without quoting** — she reuses
one boilerplate paragraph nearly word for word on inspection certificates — the
kit refused to lift it and said so:

> "You reuse one paragraph almost word for word in these. I'm not going to copy
> it into a file, because this profile is supposed to be safe to have lying
> around. If you want the drafts to include it, paste it to me yourself and I'll
> record it as your own boilerplate, marked as something you gave me."

She chose not to. Owner-supplied text is the only route by which literal writing
enters a profile, and it exists because the owner handed it over, not because
the kit took it.
